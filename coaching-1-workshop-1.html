Coaching 1: Goals

Exploring how we set goals and what can often go wrong

Goal setting is the most prescribed tool in personal development. Specific, challenging goals have been shown to boost performance across hundreds of studies.

But what if the prescription has side effects?

You are about to meet four students starting university, just like you. Each one sets a goal. Your job: decide what they should do.

Alex has just arrived at university. Their parents are proud. Their school teachers said they had potential. Alex wants to prove them all right.

In their first coaching meeting, the coach asks: “What does success look like for you this year?”

Alex thinks for a moment and says: “I want to do really well academically. That’s the priority.”

The coach asks Alex to turn this into a clear goal.

What goal should Alex set?

Vote now.

How confident are you this will work?

What actually happened

A. The 3.8 GPA: Alex hit 3.82. But by December, they had skipped every social event, dropped out of the sports club they’d joined in Week 1, and turned down a team consulting project that carried no grade weight. In January, they felt isolated, could point to no skills beyond exam technique, and had nothing to talk about in interviews except grades.

B. Three hours every evening: Alex sat at their desk reliably. But “study” became passive re-reading. The goal measured time rather than learning. By November, Alex was logging hours but retaining little; they had stopped exercising, seeing friends, or sleeping properly to protect the routine.

C. “Do my best”: Without a clear target, Alex drifted. Some weeks they worked hard; others they coasted. By mid-semester they had lost track of whether they were progressing, and felt anxious yet directionless.

The pattern: Every option failed. The source of the failure was upstream. Alex left “success at university” undefined beyond a single dimension. A single metric replaced a complex aspiration, and everything outside it was abandoned.

To the room:

What did Alex actually want? Was it really just a number?

What got sacrificed, and why did the goal leave it exposed?

Sam played a lot of sport at school and wants to stay active at university. In their coaching meeting, Sam says: “I want to get properly fit: not just dabble.”

Together with their coach, Sam sets a goal: Run a half marathon before the end of the academic year.

By week 6, Sam can run 5K comfortably. They feel good. Then life gets busy: exams, group projects, a part-time job.

What should Sam do now?

Vote now.

How confident are you this will work?

What actually happened

A. Push through: Sam forced early morning runs on too little sleep, skipped group project meetings to train, and picked up a knee injury in week 10. After two weeks off, they never went back. The fitness goal destroyed the very thing it targeted.

B. Drop it: Sam felt relieved for a week, then guilty. They’d “failed.” The experience of quitting eroded their confidence. When a friend suggested joining a climbing club, Sam thought: “What’s the point? I’ll just quit that too.”

C. Harder short-term target: The 22-minute goal added pressure to something that had been enjoyable. Runs became tests. Sam started skipping sessions they might “fail” and only ran on days they felt fast. By December they were running once a fortnight.

The pattern: Sam’s goal lacked structure between “I want to be fit” and a specific race distance. When the aggressive target collided with real life, there was nothing to fall back on. Hitting it meant overreach; missing it meant failure; and achieving the smaller milestone (5K) felt like a ceiling rather than a foundation.

To the room:

Sam could run 5K, a real achievement. Why did it feel hollow?

What was missing between “I want to be fit” and “run a half marathon”?

Jordan is a talented photographer. They’ve done it since they were 14; it’s the thing that makes them feel most like themselves. At university, Jordan sees classmates starting side projects and thinks: “I should make this count for something.”

In their coaching meeting, Jordan sets a goal: Earn £500 from photography by the end of semester one.

By week 9, Jordan has earned £310. Three weeks remain. The goal is slipping.

What should Jordan do?

Vote now.

How confident are you this will work?

What actually happened

A. Grind to £500: Jordan took three corporate jobs in two weeks. The work was tedious. They delivered acceptable but uninspired photos and started cutting corners on editing to meet deadlines. They hit £510. The camera sat untouched for the next two months. The thing they loved became the thing they resented.

B. Lower the target: Jordan hit £380 and technically “succeeded.” But it felt hollow. The adjusted goal felt like an admission of inadequacy, and the entire semester’s photography was now framed by a number rather than by growth, creativity, or identity.

C. Forget the number: Jordan felt guilty about abandoning a goal. In their next coaching meeting, they struggled to articulate what they’d achieved. Without any structure, “shoot what I love” produced great photos but zero development direction, and Jordan could not explain their progress.

The pattern: Jordan attached a financial metric to something driven by identity and love of the craft. The extrinsic target displaced the intrinsic motivation. When the number came under pressure, Jordan faced a choice between compromising their standards or compromising their goal. Every version of the £500 target carried this flaw: the target itself was the source of the conflict.

To the room:

Jordan still cares about photography. So why did every option feel wrong?

Is the issue the specific number, or is it where the goal was aimed?

Riley is president of the university Entrepreneurship Society. Membership has been flat at 40 for a year. The student union warns that funding will be cut unless numbers improve significantly.

Riley sets a goal: “Double membership to 80 by end of semester.”

By week 7, membership is at 52. The next big event is in two weeks.

What should Riley do?

Vote now.

How confident are you this will work?

What actually happened

A. Free pizza and giveaways: Membership hit 85. But 50 of those people came once for free food and never returned. Core members felt the society had lost its purpose. Next semester, active attendance dropped below 30: lower than where Riley started.

B. Counting single-event attendees: Riley reported 83 members to the student union. Funding was secured. But the number was fiction, and the whole committee knew it. A culture of “hit the metric, sort out reality later” set in. When the society needed engaged members to run a startup weekend, 12 people showed up.

C. Better events, accept the number: Events improved. Membership reached 58. The student union cut funding by 30%. Riley felt punished for doing the right thing. The lesson they internalised: integrity is penalised. They stopped volunteering for leadership roles.

The pattern: The society existed to build a community around entrepreneurship. The membership number served as a proxy for that purpose, a stand-in for the real mission. When the proxy became the goal, every path led to either gaming the metric or suffering for refusing to.

To the room:

What was the Entrepreneurship Society actually for?

How did the membership target change what Riley was willing to do?

Are SMART goals the smart choice?

Every goal was specific. Most were challenging. Several were measurable and time-bound. By every conventional standard, these were “good” goals.

Yet each one produced the same kinds of failure: tunnel vision, collapsed motivation, abandoned progress, eroded confidence, compromised standards.

The problem lay in what was missing around each goal.
